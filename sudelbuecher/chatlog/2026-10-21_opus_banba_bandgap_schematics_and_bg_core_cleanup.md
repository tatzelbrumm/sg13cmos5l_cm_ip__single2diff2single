The Banba bandgap is the **current-mode bandgap**. Instead of adding a PTAT voltage to V_BE, it adds a PTAT current and a CTAT current, then turns the sum into a voltage with a resistor. The output is a resistor ratio times the bandgap voltage, so it can sit anywhere below 1.2 V, and the supply only has to hold up V_BE plus one saturation voltage.

## Functional principle

The circuit has three matched PMOS current sources, M1–M3, all carrying the same current I. An op-amp forces node A equal to node B.

- **Branch A:** diode D1 (1×), with R1 in parallel from A to ground.
- **Branch B:** R0 in series with diode D2 (N×), with R2 = R1 in parallel from B to ground.
- **Branch 3:** M3 drives R3, and the output is taken across R3.

Since V_A = V_B = V_BE1 and R1 = R2, both resistor legs carry V_BE1/R1. With equal totals in the two branches, the diode currents must also be equal. That puts ΔV_BE = V_T·ln N across R0.

- I = V_T ln N / R0 + V_BE1 / R1, which is PTAT plus CTAT.
- V_ref = R3·I = (R3/R1)·[V_BE1 + (R1/R0)·V_T ln N] = (R3/R1)·V_BG.

You choose R1/R0 to null the first-order temperature coefficient, then choose R3/R1 to scale the output, for example 0.5 V. Only resistor ratios enter V_ref, so the resistor TC cancels to first order. The current I itself is not temperature-stable unless the resistors have zero TC.

## What's special about it

**Compared with voltage-mode bandgaps (Kuijk, Brokaw, Widlar):**

- **Headroom.** The minimum supply is about V_BE(cold) + |V_DS,sat| ≈ 0.8–0.9 V, not V_BG + headroom ≈ 1.4 V or more. That headroom is the reason the circuit exists.
- **Arbitrary output.** Any value below about V_BG works, and extra mirror legs give several scaled references or a near-constant current for free.
- **Easy curvature correction.** Because the circuit sums currents, a nonlinear third current (V_T ln T style) can simply be added at the output node.

**What it doesn't fix, and the new problems it brings:**

- **Op-amp offset.** The offset is multiplied by about R3/R0, which is about (V_ref/V_BG) × (V_BG − V_BE)/ΔV_BE. The relative error at the output is therefore the same as in the Kuijk cell. Chopping or trimming is still needed for precision.
- **Mirror matching and channel-length modulation.** Mismatch in M1–M3 adds directly to the error. M3's drain sits at V_ref while M1/M2 sit at V_BE, so V_DS differs. Cascoding fixes that but costs the headroom the circuit was meant to save.
- **Op-amp input common mode.** The inputs sit at V_BE, about 0.5–0.75 V across temperature, which is awkward at a ~1 V supply. That is the problem behind the low-V_T devices in the original and the divider trick in Leung–Mok.
- **Start-up.** There is the usual zero-current state. There is also a possible degenerate state at low current where the diodes are barely on and the resistors carry everything while the op-amp is still satisfied. A start-up circuit is mandatory, and the start-up behaviour needs checking across corners.
- **Output impedance.** V_ref comes from a current source into R3, so it is high-impedance. It needs a buffer, or the load must be included in the design.
- **PSRR** depends on the PMOS sources' output resistance and the op-amp's PSRR.
- **Resistor area at low power.** R1 ≈ V_BE / I_R1, which is about 6 MΩ at 100 nA. In nanoampere designs the high-resistance poly area dominates quickly.

**Compared with non-bandgap references:** threshold-voltage-based and ΔV_GS references (Oguey–Aebischer style, or the 2-transistor picowatt references) reach lower supply and lower power. But they rely on MOS parameters that vary much more from wafer to wafer than V_BE. The Banba cell keeps the bandgap's physical anchor and makes it fit under 1 V. In a CMOS-only process like SG13CMOS5L, it runs on the parasitic vertical PNP, the same way the Toshiba original used diodes in a flash process.

The skill is a small core: MOSFET, R/C and supply/ground symbols, plus a very simple router. I extended it in a local copy and didn't touch the skill itself.

- **Missing parts.** It has no diode, op-amp, BJT, output terminal or text-annotation elements, so I added all of them. The annotations have no pins, so they can't fake a connection.
- **Horizontal wiring.** Its connect function promises horizontal bus routing, but the drawing code ignores that and draws every net around a vertical line. A horizontal V_cc rail or gate bus was impossible until I implemented it.
- **PMOS symbol bug.** The body arrow is drawn on the gate side, on top of the gate plate, so the gate looks fused to the channel. The skill's own conventions doc warns against exactly that look. It also affects the skill's own HV-driver example. I fixed it locally.
- **Junction dots.** It put dots on plain corners and on straight two-pin joins. I changed it to dot only where three or more wires meet.

## Limits I couldn't fix cheaply

- **Wire crossings.** Each net is drawn as one line with straight branches. So PMOS bulk ties show up as wires running parallel to the source leads up to the rail, and the gate bus crosses the P1/P2 source and bulk leads.
- **No net labels.** Every connection has to be drawn as a wire. That's why the op-amp points up and sits between the two branches; Banba avoided this by labelling the inputs V_a and V_b.
- **The check is shallower than it sounds.** It only confirms that every pin is in exactly one net. Nothing checks the drawing itself, so a wire passing over an unrelated pin, or two nets drawn on top of each other, would not be caught.
- **No simulation.** There is a netlist internally, but it can't be exported to SPICE.

**Compared with xschem:** the analog-schematic skill is a figure generator for documents and slides. It is correct by construction at the netlist level, but stylistically limited. xschem gives real PDK symbols (pnpMPA, rppd), net labels, and a netlist read back from the drawing that you can simulate and compare device by device against a reference netlist. For anything that goes on to simulation, xschem is the right tool.
